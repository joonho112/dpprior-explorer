Dominance in stick-breaking rows

Catalog M-17, M-18, C-16

Fifty stick-breaking draws under a hyperprior show how often the first piece takes more than half of the stick, and why the first piece is not always the largest.

Stick-breaking builds the cluster weights one piece at a time: V_k ~ Beta(1, alpha) and w_k = V_k × (what is left of the stick). The first piece has a special reading. It is the size-biased weight W_SB: the population share of the cluster that a randomly chosen unit belongs to. Its tail has a closed form under a Gamma(a, b) hyperprior, S(t) = Pr(W_SB > t) = (b / (b − log(1 − t)))^a.

M-17 · How often does the first piece take more than half?

Each row below draws its own alpha from the hyperprior and then breaks one stick. The first piece is in colour; a red dot marks the rows where it passes one half. Press Redraw (or Enter) a few times: the running share settles near S(.5).

  • Gamma(1, 1): S(.5) = .591. More than half of the rows have a first piece above one half.
  • Gamma(1, 3): S(.5) = .812. The hyperprior puts more mass near alpha = 0, and most sticks break almost whole at the first cut.
  • Dual-Anchor Gamma(2.32, 1.42): S(.5) = .398. This is the J = 50 worked example’s Dual-Anchor prior (Part 4), which keeps “about five clusters” while bringing S(.5) under its .40 trigger.

M-18 · The first piece is not always the largest

The same draws again, each row shown twice: in breaking order (top) and sorted by size (bottom). A red dot marks rows where the first piece to break off is not the largest.

When the first piece is above one half, the other pieces share less than one half, so the first piece is the largest. The reverse fails: the largest piece can break off later. That is why Pr(W_SB > .5) is a lower bound for Pr(W_max > .5), the probability that the largest cluster holds more than half of the population.

C-16 · How far apart can W_SB and W_max be?

Proposition 1 of the paper turns the M-18 argument into a band. For t of one half or more,

S(t) ≤ Pr(W_max > t) ≤ min(1, S(t) / t),

and the engine also computes the middle term exactly (a one-dimensional integral after the substitution u = −log(1 − w)). Under Gamma(1, 1) at t = .5 the band is [.591, 1] and the exact value is .728. As t grows the two tails meet: a random unit falls in a piece larger than t with probability at least t, so at t = .9 the tails are .303 (W_SB) and .309 (W_max).

The STAR posterior switch adds the application’s released posterior summaries (paper Table 5) for the five STAR priors. They are posterior quantities, conditional on the 79 schools, and are read from the replication package, not recomputed. Under the vague Gamma(1, 1) prior the largest occupied cluster holds .920 of the schools on average and the posterior probability that the largest population weight exceeds one half is .963; under the Dual-Anchor prior these drop to .724 and .804, and the alpha-only size-biased probability drops from .797 to .419.

W_SB is the share of a random unit’s cluster, never “the largest weight”. Size-biased dominance and W_max are named separately throughout this site. These are prior statements. Stick-breaking rows after Broderick (MLSS 2016; 2012 tutorial), with the mathematics re-implemented.
